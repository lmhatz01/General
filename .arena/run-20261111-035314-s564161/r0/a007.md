# Best-suited university options for Lukas (VTAC, 2027 entry)

## Bottom line

1. **Monash, Bachelor of Engineering (Honours), Mechanical, as a single degree, with business/finance electives and full commitment to Monash Motorsport. Make this your first VTAC preference.**
2. **Monash, Engineering (Honours) + Commerce (Finance) double degree.** This is a close second, and a legitimate first preference only if you are certain finance is a real co-goal and not just insurance.
3. University of Melbourne, Bachelor of Science (Engineering Systems) then Master of Engineering (Mechanical). This is a 3+2 structure, and Melbourne University Motorsport is a strong team.
4. Swinburne, Bachelor of Engineering (Honours) (Mechanical), if the paid industry placement is a priority.
5. RMIT, Bachelor of Engineering (Honours) (Mechanical).
6. UNSW (Sydney), as the best interstate option, but only if you are prepared to relocate.
7. Deakin, then UQ, Sydney and Adelaide, as reasonable backups. They are not ranked in detail.

The double degree is not automatically better. For a motorsport-first goal it is the weaker default. Your own criteria say technical depth, WAM and Formula SAE time matter most, and a double degree takes from all three.

## Worked example: what the double degree actually costs

Take Monash as the example, because it is your main option. Assume a full load is 4 units a semester (24 credit points), which I believe is Monash's standard. Treat these as approximate and check the Handbook.

- Single engineering honours: about 32 units, so 4 years at 8 units a year.
- Double with Commerce: about 40 units, so 5 years at full load.
- Your idea of 3 units per semester: 40 units / 6 per year is about **6.7 years**. That means seven years of study, a full year or two later into the workforce than the single degree, and two or three more years of fees and HECS-HELP accrual.

What that buys you: a commerce degree with a finance major and a lighter weekly load. What it does not buy you: more time for Formula SAE in the years you most need it. Spreading the same degree over more years reduces the study per semester but extends the commitment. Formula SAE competes with study in every semester of those seven years.

Compare the single degree at 4 units a semester with about 4 units of finance/business electives inside it, if the course structure allows (check electives and any minor rules). You get roughly one-eighth of your degree in finance, the full technical core, and a standard 4-year path.

## Double vs single: the questions you asked

| Factor | Single Eng (Mech) | Eng + Commerce (Finance) |
|---|---|---|
| Technical depth | Full core plus technical electives | Core preserved, but fewer technical electives; accreditation intact |
| WAM | Easier to protect | Harder; commerce units add load and a second set of assessments |
| Formula SAE capacity | Best | Worse unless you extend the length |
| Duration | 4 years | 5 years, or about 6.7 at 3 units a semester |
| Cost | Lower | About a year more fees and HECS-HELP debt at full load, more if extended |
| Employability in motorsport | Strongest signal is Formula SAE plus WAM | Commerce adds almost nothing for F1 team hiring |
| Employability in finance | Possible, but harder for graduate banking roles that screen by degree | Clear signal, strong for finance and consulting |
| Flexibility | Can add a postgraduate finance degree later | Keeps both doors open from day one |

**Direct answer to "does engineering alone give a pathway into finance later?"** Yes, but unevenly. Engineers are recruited into consulting, quantitative roles and some corporate finance, and a postgraduate Master of Finance or an MBA later is a normal route. What you lose is the undergraduate finance credential for the most degree-screened graduate programs. That is a real cost only if you actually want those roles. The reverse path (commerce graduate into F1 engineering) does not work.

**Where your plan is weak:**
1. Finance is described as a fallback, but the fallback is costly: roughly a year or more of extra time, extra fees and a lower WAM risk for a plan B you may never use.
2. Formula SAE and WAM both drive F1 and motorsport hiring, and a double degree weakens both.
3. "Interest in motorsport" is not a skill. F1 teams hire on technical ability, so the best evidence is projects you built, designed and tested.
4. Be honest about the odds: F1 team roles are few and global. A strong mechanical engineering degree with Formula SAE experience also leads to Supercars, Tier 1 automotive, aerospace, defence and high-performance engineering, which is your realistic safety net. That safety net is better than finance for a technical student, and it does not need a commerce degree.

**What would make the double degree rank first:** you realise finance interests you as much as motorsport; you are comfortable with a likely lower WAM; or you decide not to join Monash Motorsport.

## Skill development by pathway (what actually builds what)

Distinguishing three things: **direct skill** (graded or built), **transferable intuition**, and **mere interest**.

- **Direct skill, from the degree:** mechanics and dynamics (vehicle dynamics, suspension kinematics), mathematics and numerical methods (simulation, optimisation), materials, thermofluids and aerodynamics (CFD basics), control, manufacturing, and engineering design. Look for the units covering each in the Monash Handbook, and check unit codes there, not in my notes.
- **Direct skill, from Monash Motorsport (not graded):** parametric CAD, assemblies, drawings, tolerancing and design for manufacture and assembly, basic FEA, composites, machining, data acquisition, documentation and teamwork. This is where most of your CAD and practical skill will come from. Course CAD classes are introductory.
- **Transferable intuition:** car setup, driving and watching racing give you intuition, which helps conversations but is not hiring evidence.
- **Mere interest:** following F1 counts for nothing on its own.

For subject choices, favour mathematics and numerical methods, dynamics, control, materials, manufacturing, CAD-based design, and data analysis. Take Monash's finance electives (if the course allows) only after the technical electives are secured.

## Comparing the options

For every university: **verify ATAR, prerequisites, scheme/adjustment factors, fees and course structure on the official page before ranking.**

1. **Monash (single).** Large, well-resourced Victorian engineering school, a very successful and well-known Formula SAE team (Monash Motorsport), strong industry links, close to Melbourne's automotive and motorsport scene. A 4-year honours degree. Best fit overall.
2. **Monash (double).** Same team access with a heavier workload. Check whether you can transfer between single and double after first year; I have not verified that.
3. **Melbourne.** Prestigious, strong maths and science base, a strong team (Melbourne University Motorsport). The 3+2 model is longer (5 years) and the Master's year costs more. Higher selection and a broad first degree. Good alternative if you want a research-oriented path or prestige, but it is a weaker fit for a hands-on, 4-year track.
4. **Swinburne.** Strong applied and industry-linked profile, a Formula SAE team, and an industry-based learning (placement) option. Good for practical experience and employability. Check placement details and the team's recent results.
5. **RMIT.** Strong hands-on, industry-connected engineering with a motorsport team. Check unit details and the facilities for fabrication.
6. **UNSW.** Strong engineering brand, Redback Racing, but requires interstate relocation, which costs money and time. Worth it only if you get a strong offer.
7. **Deakin, UQ, Sydney, Adelaide.** Credible but less compelling for your stated goals, given distance or fewer apparent advantages. Adelaide's recent merger adds uncertainty.
8. **Overseas (UK, e.g. Oxford Brookes, Loughborough, Cranfield).** Not recommended for undergraduate study: international fees, no HECS-HELP, visa and living costs. They are more realistic later as a Master's if you have a clear reason, for example motorsport-specific postgraduate study.
