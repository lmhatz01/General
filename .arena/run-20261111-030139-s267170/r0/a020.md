## First principles: what actually gets someone into F1 or motorsport engineering
Only a few things are not in dispute:
1. A recognised mechanical (or similar) engineering degree. This is the entry ticket.
2. Demonstrated technical output: a design you built and tested, a car that ran. Formula SAE (FSAE) is the clearest evidence of this.
3. Strong grades. They matter most for the first job screen, and F1 teams and top Tier-1 suppliers are very selective.
4. Practical skills: CAD, manufacture, data acquisition and analysis.

Finance and commerce are not on this list. Under your stated priorities, any course choice should therefore be judged on how much it raises 1 to 4, and the double degree on how much it costs them.

Most F1 teams are in the UK, and overseas masters or UK-based roles are a later step. Do not pick an undergraduate university for that. An Australian degree plus FSAE is a normal route, though it is highly competitive.

## The double-degree question
Answer: **for your stated primary goal, the single engineering degree with finance electives is the better default. The double degree is justified only if finance is a real career option for you, not just a hedge.**

Reasoning:
- **Technical depth:** a double degree usually means fewer engineering electives or a longer timeline. Your motorsport skills (CAD, vehicle dynamics, manufacturing) come from engineering subjects and FSAE, not commerce.
- **WAM:** a commerce load adds subjects that are not your strength profile. Your VCE mix (Methods, Physics, Economics) fits engineering well, and Economics gives some commerce footing. Extra subjects mean more chances to lower your average. Whether that matters depends on how F1 recruiters weigh WAM, which is uncertain, but graduate selection generally screens on it.
- **FSAE capacity:** FSAE is a large time commitment. Spreading the double degree to about three units a semester frees some time but adds roughly two years and more cost. A two-year extension delays your earnings and your entry into motorsport.
- **Employability and flexibility:** an engineering degree already opens doors into finance (quant, analytics, consulting, some banking graduate programs), and many employers in those fields recruit engineers. It is not as direct as a commerce degree, and some finance roles do filter on it, so say so plainly. But the reverse is harder: commerce graduates cannot become accredited engineers without further study.
- **Opportunity cost and duration:** the double degree is 5 years at full load (check exact length), and longer if spread. HECS-HELP lets you defer the cost, but you still owe it. More years means more debt and later full-time income. Verify current HECS indexation and cost per unit on the Monash site.
- **Switching later:** many universities let you add or drop the second degree in the first year or two. Check Monash's rules. If true, start single and add commerce later if finance becomes real. That keeps the option cheap.

What would change this: if you decide finance is equally likely as motorsport, or you do not get into a motorsport team, the double degree becomes more attractive.

## Subjects and skills
Codes below are from memory and **must be checked in the Monash Handbook**. I do not know what MMA3101 is. Look it up before choosing it.

What to favour, and why it matters:
- **Engineering mathematics and numerical methods:** the basis for simulation and lap-time modelling.
- **Dynamics and vibrations:** the core of suspension and vehicle dynamics. Directly applicable to kinematics and damper tuning.
- **Control and mechatronics:** relevant for powertrain, traction control and data acquisition.
- **Materials and manufacturing:** needed for composites, machining and design for manufacture.
- **Fluid mechanics and thermodynamics:** aerodynamics and powertrain.
- **Engineering design and CAD subjects:** build parametric modelling, assemblies, drawings and tolerancing. FSAE will teach more of this than most coursework, so treat the team as your CAD and DFM lab.
- **Finance electives (if any):** choose one or two that fit a single degree. Do not treat them as technical skill.

Skill categories, kept distinct:
- **Direct engineering skill:** dynamics, materials, CAD, FEA and control, built through coursework and FSAE design work.
- **Transferable practical intuition:** workshop time, machining, composites layup, tuning a car, learned mainly in the team.
- **Mere interest:** watching F1 or following the sport. It helps motivation but does not count as evidence for recruiters.

## Where your plan is weak
- Assuming the double degree is a safe hedge. It costs time, WAM risk and FSAE capacity, which are exactly what your primary goal needs.
- Treating FSAE as automatic. Teams select members, and the demands are heavy. Contact the team early.
- Relying on an ATAR figure you do not have. Selection ranks and adjustment factors change each year, and I cannot confirm them.
- Picking a university for the F1 link alone. Very few graduates reach F1 directly, so choose a degree that is also good if you end up elsewhere in engineering.

## What would change the ranking
- You miss the Monash cut-off or a team place: Melbourne, RMIT or Swinburne move up.
- You discover you prefer finance: the double degree moves to rank 1.
- A course check shows Monash's single degree has limited elective room: reconsider the double degree or a different university.
- A university offers a scholarship, adjustment scheme or placement that changes the economics.
